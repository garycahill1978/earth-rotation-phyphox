Measuring the Earth's Rotation with phyphox

Can an ordinary smartphone directly measure the rotation of the Earth?

Gary Cahill
South East Technological University, Ireland

Where does 0.00418° per second come from?

Earth's rotation rate ≈ 0.00418°/s
1

Earth moves around the Sun as well as spinning. In one day it moves about

\[ \frac{360^\circ}{365} \approx 1^\circ \]

around its orbit.

2

This makes one rotation relative to the stars about 4 minutes shorter than a 24-hour solar day:

\[ \frac{24\ \mathrm{h}}{365} \approx 4\ \mathrm{min} \]
3

A sidereal day is approximately

\[ 23\ \mathrm{h}\; 56\ \mathrm{min}\; 4\ \mathrm{s} \]

giving

\[ \Omega_E = \frac{360^\circ} {86164\ \mathrm{s}} \approx 0.00418^\circ\!/\mathrm{s} \]
That is the tiny angular velocity the phone gyroscope is trying to detect — and it can easily be hidden by sensor noise, bias and drift.

Smartphone gyroscope axes and the horizontal and vertical components of the Earth's rotation.

A phone pointed North (+y North) measures the horizontal component of Earth's rotation on its +y axis. A phone lying flat at the North Pole measures Earth's rotation on its +z axis. At intermediate latitudes, the signal is shared between the +y and +z components.

The ABBA reversal method

Measurements are made in one orientation, after a 180° reversal, and then again after returning to the original orientation.

A1 → B1 → B2 → A2
\[ \Delta\omega = \frac{A_1+A_2}{2} - \frac{B_1+B_2}{2} \]

Why ABBA?

A 180° reversal changes the sign of the Earth-rotation signal, while the constant gyroscope bias does not change.

The ABBA sequence is also symmetric in time:

\[ \frac{t_{A1}+t_{A2}}{2} = \frac{t_{B1}+t_{B2}}{2} \]

The average measurement time is the same for A and B, suppressing approximately linear gyroscope drift.

180° reversal cancels constant bias  •  ABBA timing suppresses linear drift

Is your phone gyroscope good enough?

The Earth-rotation signal is extremely small. Even if a gyroscope looks quiet from one reading to the next, its average can slowly wander with time. We need to know how stable that average is over the 5–20 second intervals used in the experiment.

The Android phones use the uncalibrated gyroscope stream, while the iPhone 13 and iPad Air use the standard calibrated gyroscope stream available to phyphox on iOS. The vertical markers show 5, 10, 15 and 20 s averaging times. The dashed line marks the expected horizontal Earth-rotation component near Ireland, approximately 0.0025° s⁻¹.

What does the comparison show? The smartphone gyroscopes become more stable as the averaging time increases through the 5–20 s region. The Murata SCH16T-K01 is substantially more stable over the same averaging times, providing a useful reference for the smartphone measurements.

The iPad Air (1st generation, 2013; iOS 12.5.8) is included as an example of a mobile device whose gyroscope is not sufficiently stable for this experiment. Its Allan deviation remains above the expected horizontal Earth-rotation signal throughout the averaging times shown, demonstrating that sensor performance varies substantially between devices.

From atomic clocks to phone gyroscopes

Allan deviation is named after physicist David W. Allan and was developed for precision measurements of time and frequency.

It is used to study the stability of extremely precise oscillators and atomic clocks, and has also been used to characterise the clocks carried by GPS satellites.

Here we use the same idea for a smartphone gyroscope: how stable is the measured rotation rate when we average for different lengths of time?

Standard deviation

asks:

“How noisy are the individual readings?”

Allan deviation

asks:

“How stable is the average if I measure for this long?”

What does it actually do?

Choose an averaging time \( \tau \). Divide the gyro data into intervals of that length, calculate the average rotation rate in each interval, and compare neighbouring averages.

\[ \sigma_A(\tau) = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{2} \left\langle \left( \bar{\omega}_{i+1} - \bar{\omega}_{i} \right)^2 \right\rangle } \]

If neighbouring averages are very similar, the Allan deviation is small and the sensor is stable at that averaging time.

Two different timescales matter

It is useful to separate the averaging time for each individual gyro measurement from the total duration of the full ABBA sequence. They affect the experiment in different ways.

1. Individual averaging time, \( \tau \)

This is the duration of each A or B measurement. Increasing \( \tau \) reduces short-term random noise and usually improves the stability of the average.

The Allan-deviation plot shows this directly: the smartphone curves fall strongly as the averaging time increases through the first few tens of seconds.

2. Total ABBA sequence time

Longer individual measurements also make the whole A1–B1–B2–A2 sequence longer.

Over a longer experiment, slow gyroscope bias changes, temperature drift and other low-frequency effects have more time to influence the result.

Why use 15-second measurements?

A 15 s averaging interval is a practical compromise, not necessarily the minimum point on the Allan-deviation curve.

By 15 s, much of the short-term random noise has already been reduced, while the complete ABBA sequence can still be finished in about 76 s. This keeps the full reversal experiment below roughly 100 s, limiting the time available for slow drift and thermal changes to build up.

So the aim is not simply to make each measurement as long as possible: average long enough to reduce noise, but keep the complete experiment short enough to suppress slow drift.

Longer \( \tau \) → less random noise  •  Longer full sequence → more exposure to slow drift

Can the gyro resolve the Earth signal?

Comparing the Allan deviation with the expected Earth-rotation signal provides a practical indication of whether a gyroscope is stable enough to attempt the measurement. The ABBA reversal experiment then tests whether the signal is actually retained and detected.

The sensor stream also matters. Comparing calibrated and uncalibrated gyroscope streams is important because software bias compensation can alter very slow changes in the signal — exactly the behaviour we are trying to measure.
